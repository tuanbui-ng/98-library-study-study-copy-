Method

How the readers are graded

Every reader in this library was written for one level and then checked with a lexical profiler against Paul Nation’s BNC/COCA word family lists. A reader is published only if at least 98% of its running words (tokens) belong to families within its level, with names and the supplementary lists counted as known, and every family beyond the level appears in its glossary.

The word lists

The BNC/COCA lists (Nation, 2017, Version 1.0.0) contain 25 lists of 1,000 word families ranked by frequency and range, plus four supplementary lists: proper names, marginal words (exclamations, swear words, letters of the alphabet), transparent compounds, and acronyms. The first two 1,000 lists come from a 10-million-word corpus that is 60% spoken English, which brings everyday words like hello, dad and station into the first 1,000. The 3rd 1,000 onward come from combined BNC and COCA rankings.

Families follow Bauer and Nation’s (1993) Level 6: a headword plus its inflections and derived forms built with affixes from Levels 2–6, so happy, happily, happiness and unhappy form one family. For readers at K1–K3 the library also reports coverage with Nation’s Level 3 partial lists, which keep only inflections and four transparent affixes (un-, -ly, -er, ordinal -th). That unit is closer to what beginners can recognise, and it gives a second, stricter check.

The levels

Each level adds one 1,000-family band. Levels 1–3 cover the high-frequency vocabulary that graded readers traditionally teach. Levels 4–8 move into mid-frequency vocabulary, in the spirit of Nation and Anthony’s (2013) mid-frequency readers, so that learners can keep reading with 98% coverage while they build their vocabulary towards the 8,000–9,000 families needed for unadapted text.

Glossaries list every word family beyond the level under Level 6 counting. The word count at the end of each story is a conventional count, where contractions and hyphenated words count as one word. The coverage profile counts running words the way Range does, so don’t is two words there.

Why 98%

Hu and Nation (2000) found that most learners needed about 98% known words to read fiction with adequate comprehension without help, which is one unknown word in fifty. Laufer (1989) proposed 95% as a minimum, and Schmitt, Jiang and Grabe (2011) found that comprehension rises steadily with coverage, with 98% a reasonable target for comfortable reading. Nation (2006) estimated that 8,000–9,000 families give 98% coverage of written text, which is why unadapted texts are so hard for most learners and why graded and mid-frequency readers are needed.

Counting rules

  • A word is a run of letters, as in Range and AntWordProfiler. Apostrophes and hyphens split words: don’t becomes don + t, both listed in the 1st 1,000. Numbers written as digits are not counted.
  • Accents are ignored when matching (café matches cafe).
  • Words in the four supplementary lists count as known. So do each story’s own character and place names when they are capitalised.
  • Short phrases in another language, such as the Vietnamese and Māori lines in two stories, are shown in italics and left out of the count.
  • A word form that appears in more than one family is assigned to the most frequent one. The lists count forms, not senses, so homographs such as left (direction, past of leave) share a band.
  • Coverage measures vocabulary only. It does not capture grammar, background knowledge or the difficulty of the ideas, which were controlled by writing to each level.
